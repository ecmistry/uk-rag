# The Health of UK High Streets
## A free-source regional and sector review

**Briefing for Rory — rebuilt September 2026**

**Evidence rule:** every headline figure in this edition is available without buying a dataset or joining a trade body. Sources are ONS, councils, devolved governments, Open Data NI, free BID reports, public registers, open research, and freely published property-company notes.

---

## Executive conclusion

UK high streets are **rebalancing rather than uniformly collapsing**. Savills, citing Green Street, reported GB high-street vacancy at **13.2% in Q1 2026**, down from 13.4% in Q4 2025; shopping-centre vacancy was higher at **16.9%**.[1] The national improvement conceals severe local voids: Peterlee **40.8%**, Bishop Auckland **35.4%**, Hanley **30.4%**, Leicester **22.1%**, Elgin **20%**, Carmarthen **19%**, and Reading **18.6% of units** in their respective council studies.[2][3][4][5][6][7]

The recurring evidence is stronger for **footfall** than for vacancy. Calculations from the free ONS monthly workbook show Q2 2026 town-and-city-centre footfall was below Q2 2025 in nine of the eleven published GB regions, while London rose 8.0% and Scotland rose 0.7%. Wales fell 9.1% and the West Midlands 8.5%.[8] The series begins in July 2024, is not seasonally adjusted, excludes local residents from counts, and suppresses Northern Ireland; it should be read as a pulse, not a long-term league table.

Vape shops, barbers and charity shops are **not one “filler” category**:

- **Vape shops:** England’s open OS Points of Interest evidence shows an almost **1,200% rise from 2014 to 2024**, with twice the density in the North as the South and more than three times the provision in the most deprived areas.[9] They occupy units, but youth access and illicit products make them an enforcement and public-health issue.
- **Barbers and beauty:** official business counts support growth in the combined hair, barber, nail and beauty economy, but there is no free UK high-street barber census. Council audits show these businesses can dominate retail-service space: Elgin recorded **20 hairdresser/barber units** in 2025.[5]
- **Charity shops:** the Charity Retail Association’s public page records **9,900+ UK shops, 24,900+ FTE staff, 223,500+ volunteers and £300m+ profit to parent charities in 2024/25**.[10] They provide social value and occupied frontage, but are not proof of strong commercial demand.

**Practical judgement:** vacancy going down is positive, but a healthy centre also needs essential services, a diverse use mix, repeat footfall, viable local businesses and effective enforcement. If pharmacies and banks disappear while vape and takeaway uses cluster, occupancy may improve while health worsens.

## 1. What this free evidence can measure

| Question | Best free source | Cadence | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Are visits rising? | ONS UK retail footfall (BT Active Intelligence) | Weekly/monthly | From July 2024; not seasonally adjusted; NI suppressed |
| Is GB vacancy improving? | Savills/Knight Frank public notes citing Green Street | Quarterly headline when published | GB headline, not a free regional table |
| Which named towns have empty units? | Council health checks and retail studies | Six-monthly to annual, irregular | Boundaries and methods differ |
| What is happening in NI? | LPS Open Data NI | Quarterly | All non-domestic property, not shop-only |
| How are high-street jobs changing? | ONS BRES high-street dataset | Periodic/annual source data | Employment, not occupancy |
| How is the use mix changing? | Council audits, CRA public stats, HEN/OS POI, Scotland NVP register | Mixed | Categories and geographies are incompatible |
| Where does resident spending leak? | ONS/Visa postal-district flow data | Latest published data only to Q3 2023 | Not a dependable quarterly publication |

There is **no free, comparable quarterly shop-vacancy rate for every UK region or town**, and no free live catchment-spend series. This report therefore separates:

1. a recurring national and regional **footfall pulse**;
2. a recurring **GB property headline**;
3. a **town evidence ledger** updated whenever councils publish;
4. sector evidence updated at its natural cadence.

## 3. Region-by-region free evidence

### North East England

**Quarterly pulse:** town-and-city-centre footfall was **5.1% lower in Q2 2026 than Q2 2025**.[8]

**Council evidence:** Durham County Council’s 2025 surveys, reported by the BBC, put town-centre vacancy at **19.1% overall**. Peterlee was **40.8%**, Bishop Auckland **35.4%**, Newton Aycliffe 26.0%, while Seaham was 8.4% and Crook 8.8%.[2] Durham City was 11.7%. This is one of the clearest demonstrations that variation inside a region is greater than any regional average suggests.

**Mix:** Health Equity North reports North East takeaways up 35% from 2014 to 2024, banks down 30%, pharmacies down 16% and public toilets down 32%.[9] North East Trading Standards found 44% of a small 32-retailer test-purchase sample sold vapes to an underage volunteer and seized 1.4 tonnes of illegal disposable products in 2022 operations.[12]

**Conclusion:** priority is not simply filling units; it is restoring essential amenities and enforcement capacity in the weakest towns.

### North West England

**Quarterly pulse:** town-and-city-centre footfall was **4.2% lower in Q2 2026 than Q2 2025**.[8]

**Open local evidence:** Manchester BID’s free reports show how a managed core can monitor monthly visitors, but its counters cover the BID footprint, not the region.[13] Blackpool, Preston, Blackburn and Bolton appear among the higher-density vape localities in open reporting of the HEN/OS study.[14]

**Chain market:** PwC’s freely published 2024 regional release recorded **956 multiple-store openings and 1,311 closures**, net −355, for the North West. It covers chains with five or more outlets and all retail locations, not independents or high streets alone.[15]

**Conclusion:** Manchester’s managed centre and event economy should not be treated as a proxy for coastal Lancashire or district centres. A current free regional vacancy rate is unavailable.

### Yorkshire and the Humber

**Quarterly pulse:** town-and-city-centre footfall was **6.6% lower in Q2 2026 than Q2 2025**.[8]

**Open local evidence:** Leeds’ authority monitoring and Visit Leeds reporting show a strong regional centre, while Bradford’s own 2023/24 monitoring report says updated centre assessments were still pending.[16] Claims that Bradford leaks spend to Leeds and Sheffield to Meadowhall remain useful structural explanations from Centre for Cities, but its July 2025 city vacancy estimates are one-off and partly modelled, so they are not current KPIs.[17]

**Chain market:** PwC recorded **857 openings and 1,182 closures** among multiples in 2024, net −325.[15]

**Conclusion:** monitor Leeds and Sheffield footfall, but use fresh council surveys—not modelled city rankings—for vacancy decisions.

### East Midlands

**Quarterly pulse:** town-and-city-centre footfall was **5.9% lower in Q2 2026 than Q2 2025**.[8]

**Council evidence:** Nottingham’s July 2024 health check recorded **21.1% vacant units**, or 16.3% excluding Broadmarsh redevelopment units.[18] Leicester’s study recorded **22.1% of units vacant** and 23.1% of comparison floorspace.[4] Derby recorded **18.2% of outlets vacant** and annual footfall above 60 million in 2024, still below 65.5 million in 2019.[19] Newark recorded 12.63% vacancy in 2024.[20]

**Conclusion:** redevelopment voids must be separated from market vacancy. Nottingham shows how one large scheme can distort a city headline.

### West Midlands

**Quarterly pulse:** town-and-city-centre footfall was **8.5% lower in Q2 2026 than Q2 2025**, the weakest English-region change in the quarter.[8]

**Council evidence:** Hanley’s July 2025 health check recorded **154 vacant outlets, 30.4% of units and 27.6% of floorspace**.[3] Wolverhampton recorded **25.4% of outlets vacant** in 2023/24 and Walsall’s primary shopping area 25.3%; the Black Country study cautions that regeneration and large closed units inflate some totals.[21] Coventry’s January 2024 survey recorded 16.0% vacancy across its prime area, but 46.6% in City Centre South during redevelopment.[22]

**Mix:** Much Wenlock’s 2024 council survey recorded only **7% vacancy**, three hair/beauty businesses and one charity shop—an important small-town contrast.[23]

**Conclusion:** Hanley and Black Country cores require structural reduction and reuse of retail space; a single West Midlands average would hide healthier centres.

### East of England

**Quarterly pulse:** town-and-city-centre footfall was **4.1% lower in Q2 2026 than Q2 2025**.[8]

**Council evidence:** Norwich’s 2025 monitor recorded **15.1% vacant town-centre units** and 14.4% vacant floorspace when all town-centre uses were included.[24] Ipswich’s 2024/25 survey recorded **19.0% vacant units** across the central shopping area.[25] Grays’ October 2024 health check recorded 13.4% vacancy in its core shopping area.[26]

**Conclusion:** the region contains strong knowledge-economy centres and stressed traditional cores. Free council data supports town comparison, not a single regional vacancy figure.

### London

**Quarterly pulse:** London town-and-city-centre footfall was **8.0% higher in Q2 2026 than Q2 2025**.[8]

**Council evidence:** Wandsworth’s 2025 survey covered about 2,500 properties. Town-centre vacancy fell from 8.1% to **7.9%**; Wandsworth town centre fell from 15% to 13%, while Putney rose from 11% to 12%.[27]

**Central activity:** TfL reported central-London pedestrian flows in July–September 2024 at about 90% of 2019.[28] Outer and local high streets often benefit more from resident demand than commuter-dependent central pitches.

**Conclusion:** London’s footfall recovery is real in the ONS series, but borough and pitch differences remain substantial.

### South East England

**Quarterly pulse:** town-and-city-centre footfall was **5.3% lower in Q2 2026 than Q2 2025**.[8]

**Council evidence:** Reading’s health check recorded **131 vacant outlets, 18.6% of units, and 27.1% of floorspace** in May 2024, with large-format voids central to the result.[7] Winchester’s 2024 town-centre study appendices show active requirements from charity retailers, illustrating continued demand for some secondary units.[29]

**Open property evidence:** free agent reports describe tight prime South East markets, but their local rates use different portfolios and definitions. They should not be compared directly with Reading’s Goad survey.

**Conclusion:** affluent catchment income does not prevent oversupply or large-unit obsolescence.

### South West England

**Quarterly pulse:** town-and-city-centre footfall was **5.6% lower in Q2 2026 than Q2 2025**.[8]

**Council evidence:** Mid Devon’s health checks recorded Tiverton vacancy at about **8.4%** in 2022/23 and map service uses, including barbers, in centres such as Crediton.[30] Bath and North East Somerset’s free Vacant Units Action Project documents meanwhile uses and activation rather than a uniform vacancy series.[31]

**Conclusion:** tourism and seasonality make quarter-on-quarter comparisons risky; use year-on-year footfall and local audits together.

### Wales

**Quarterly pulse:** town-and-city-centre footfall was **9.1% lower in Q2 2026 than Q2 2025**.[8]

**National evidence:** Welsh Government’s 2025 *Filling the gaps* report says about **one in six shops** is empty and persistent vacancy was **7.7% in 2023**; these figures are contextual, not a live quarterly index.[32]

**Council evidence:** Cardiff’s evidence recorded **17.4% vacancy** in its central shopping area in Q4 2024, with district/local centres around 9% on average.[33] Carmarthen recorded **19% vacancy** in March 2024.[6] Monmouthshire’s 2024 survey recorded Abergavenny at **9.4%** and Caldicot’s central shopping area at 9%.[34]

**Conclusion:** Wales needs a coordinated open town-centre monitoring standard. Current evidence is strong locally but not synchronised nationally.

### Scotland

**Quarterly pulse:** town-and-city-centre footfall was **0.7% higher in Q2 2026 than Q2 2025**.[8]

**Council evidence:** Moray’s summer 2025 survey recorded **50 vacant Elgin units (20%)**, up from 40 units (16%) in 2023; the closed St Giles Centre accounted for 26 vacancies. Forres had only seven vacant units, down more than 60% from 2023.[5] Dumfries and Galloway’s 2023 health checks recorded a 15% regional-town average and 18% in Dumfries, using its own centre boundaries.[35]

**Nicotine retail:** Scotland’s mandatory register provides a free outlet base. A Scottish Government May 2026 impact assessment recorded **11,586 active registrations**: 3,100 tobacco-only, 6,781 tobacco-and-vape, and 1,705 vape-only.[36] These are registered outlets, including convenience and supermarkets—not specialist high-street vape shops.

**Conclusion:** combine ONS footfall, council health checks and the NVP register; do not substitute the vacant-and-derelict-land survey for shop vacancy.

### Northern Ireland

**Quarterly property pulse:** NI non-domestic vacancy fell from **15.8% in March 2026 to 15.3% in June**; Belfast fell from 20.6% to 19.5%.[11]

**Town evidence:** Derry/Londonderry’s 2021 council study recorded **62 vacant units out of 450 (13.7%)** and 12 charity shops.[37] Newtownards’ 2025 study found **21.3% commercial vacancy across the study area but only 5.2% on the High Street core**, illustrating a strong core-fringe split.[38]

**Conclusion:** LPS is the UK’s best free quarterly vacancy dataset, but its broad property definition means it must be paired with less frequent town-centre walkovers.

## 4. Sector contribution and limitations

### Vape shops

**Contribution:** specialist retailers and convenience outlets pay rent, support some employment and can advise adult smokers switching away from cigarettes.

**Evidence of risk:** HEN/OS records the large and unequal rise in England.[9] ASH’s 2024 GB youth survey found **48% of current 11–17-year-old vapers cited shops as a source** and 55% of young people aware of vaping had seen promotion in shops.[39] Trading Standards evidence shows underage sales and illegal products remain material.[12]

**Assessment:** count vape units separately from convenience stores; record proximity to schools and enforcement outcomes. Do not count their presence as an unqualified regeneration success.

### Barbers and personal services

**Contribution:** they are labour-intensive, difficult to move online, create repeat visits and use small units.

**Evidence boundary:** ONS SIC 96.02 counts hairdressing and beauty enterprises, not barbers alone and not high-street frontage.[40] CSJ’s open analysis of official business counts reports the combined barber, nail and beauty category up 22% since 2016, but it should not be presented as a barber-only statistic.[41] Elgin’s 20 units demonstrate local importance.[5]

**Assessment:** use council retail-service counts locally. Do not claim a national “barber boom” from proprietary unit data.

### Charity shops

**Contribution:** CRA’s public totals demonstrate employment, volunteering, reuse, affordable goods and more than £300m returned to parent charities in 2024/25.[10]

**Nuance:** HEN’s England POI series found charity-shop provision per 10,000 people fell **21% from 2014 to 2024**, so the story is not endless expansion.[9] Rates relief helps occupy marginal units but means an occupied charity unit does not necessarily indicate strong commercial rental demand.

**Assessment:** treat charity retail as social infrastructure and an occupier, while separately tracking commercial demand.

## 5. Free quarterly monitoring template

### Every quarter

1. Download the latest **ONS UK retail footfall XLSX**.
2. Average the three monthly index values in the quarter and compare with the same quarter a year earlier.
3. Report GB regions for **town and city centres**, district/local centres and retail parks separately.
4. Download the latest **LPS NI non-domestic vacancy CSV**.
5. Check for a new free **Savills, Knight Frank or Green Street property note**.
6. Search each priority council for a new health check, retail survey or authority monitoring report.
7. Update CRA public statistics, HEN/ASH evidence or enforcement figures only when a new open release appears.

### Minimum dashboard fields

| Field | Required note |
|---|---|
| Period | Survey month/quarter and publication date |
| Geography | Exact town-centre, BID or council boundary |
| Unit vacancy | Vacant units / surveyed units |
| Floorspace vacancy | Record separately from unit vacancy |
| Regeneration voids | Exclude or show a second rate |
| Footfall | Index/source; YoY preferred |
| Mix | Comparison, convenience, food, service, charity, vape, barber, vacant |
| Essential amenities | Bank, pharmacy, supermarket, public toilet |
| Enforcement | Vape test purchases, seizures, closure orders |
| Source quality | Official/council, open property note, BID, or one-off model |

### Priority free-source places

- **Distress cases:** Peterlee, Bishop Auckland, Hanley, Leicester, Elgin, Carmarthen, Reading.
- **Contrasts:** Seaham/Crook within County Durham; Forres vs Elgin; Wandsworth vs Putney; Cardiff centre vs district centres; Newtownards core vs fringe.
- **Structural competition:** Bradford/Leeds, Sheffield/Meadowhall and Bristol/Cribbs may remain qualitative until a current open spend-flow dataset appears.

---

## 6. Limitations

- There is no free quarterly shop-vacancy series for all UK regions.
- ONS footfall starts in July 2024 and is not seasonally adjusted.
- NI LPS covers all non-domestic rated property, not retail or high-street units alone.
- Council surveys use different boundaries, dates and definitions.
- Property-company headline vacancy is national and based on proprietary underlying data, although the headline note is free.
- Centre for Cities 2025 vacancy estimates are one-off and partly modelled; they are context, not monitoring.
- Vape, barber and charity datasets use incompatible classifications and must not be merged into a “filler index”.
- Catchment spending cannot be updated quarterly from free published data.
