# The Health of UK High Streets: Regional and Sector Review

**Briefing for Rory**  
September 2026  
UK nations and regions, with representative towns and cities

---

## Scope and how to read this briefing

This note covers all **12 UK nations and English regions**. It is **not** a census of every town. Comparable public vacancy, footfall and mix data do not exist for every locality. Where a figure is **GB-wide or UK-wide**, that is stated. Where evidence is **local** (council health check, BID, LPS, Centre for Cities city estimate), it should not be treated as the regional average.

The three sectors in the brief — **vape shops, barbers and charity shops** — occupy units, generate some trips and jobs, and are often treated as a single “high-street filler” story. The evidence does **not** support treating them as equivalent. Charity retail has the strongest documented social and employment contribution. Barbers are the clearest growth category in GB openings data. Vape shops have filled voids, especially in the North and in deprived areas, but they are a **health and enforcement** issue as much as a retail one.

**Core argument:** occupancy is not the same as high-street health. Filling empty units with personal services, charity retail or nicotine shops can reduce visible vacancy without restoring banks, pharmacies, toilets, comparison retail or catchment spend. Policy that only “fills shops” will miss the north–south and within-region splits that dominate the data.

## 1. Executive summary

1. **GB high-street vacancy remains structurally high.** Local Data Company tracking of the top 650 town centres put **high-street vacancy at 14.0% in 2023**, up from 13.8% the previous year; shopping centres improved while high streets did not. Persistent (long-term) high-street vacancy was about **5.3% of units**. Retail parks remained far tighter. Knight Frank cited high-street vacancy still around **14.0% into H2 2024**. These are GB/UK commercial monitors, not ONS occupancy statistics.

2. **The geography of emptiness is stubborn.** In BRC–LDC regional commentary, **Greater London, the South East and East of England** have repeatedly had the **lowest** vacancy; the **North East** the **highest**, followed by **Wales and Scotland**. Q4 2023 LDC analysis put the North East at about **17.9%**. London Assembly citing LDC put Greater London at **10.6%** in Q4 2023 versus **14.0% GB**. Wales: Welsh Government/WRC — about **one in six** shops empty; **7.7%** persistent vacancy in 2023. Northern Ireland is **not** in most LDC GB series; LPS non-domestic vacancy is a different (broader) measure and is high, especially in Belfast.

3. **Retail jobs have left central high streets.** ONS (March 2026, official statistics in development) finds **retail employment on central high streets in Great Britain fell 19% between 2015 and 2024**, versus **6%** on non-central high streets and **2%** in retail parks. Central shopping-centre retail jobs fell **31%**. Accommodation and food services employment on central high streets **rose 18%**. The mix is shifting from shops to hospitality — nationally, not only in the North.

4. **Closures remain elevated.** The Centre for Retail Research counted **13,479 UK store closures in 2024** (about 37 a day), with independents the large majority. PwC/LDC chain data show **high streets as the weakest location type** in northern England in 2024 (around **−2.9%** net chain change) while some retail parks grew.

5. **Footfall is weak and weather-sensitive.** BRC–Sensormatic: **UK footfall −2.2% in 2024**, second consecutive annual decline; high streets weaker than parks. Wales **−2.9%**; Scotland **−2.0%**; Northern Ireland **−2.2%** (NIRC). ONS BT Active Intelligence (from July 2024) shows large month-to-month regional swings; it is **not seasonally adjusted** and should not be over-interpreted as a structural ranking.

6. **Within every region, towns diverge more than regions diverge from each other.** Illustrations (different methodologies; not a league table): Peterlee **40.8%** town-centre vacancy (County Durham 2025); Hanley **30.4%** (July 2025); Wigan **~32%** during Galleries demolition; Leicester **22.1%** units; Nottingham **21.1%** (16.3% excluding Broadmarsh); Bradford **~18%** city-centre estimate (Centre for Cities); Newport **~19%** (highest in that 63-place set); Aberdeen council town-centre **20.7%**; Reading **18.6%** units / **27.1%** floorspace; versus Cambridge main centre **7.7%**, London city centre **~7%**, Flude agent rates in the **low single digits** for several Sussex/Hampshire primes, and Comhairle nan Eilean Siar **3.3%**.

7. **Vape shops, barbers and charity shops are not one story.**
   - **Vape shops (England):** Health Equity North / OS Points of Interest — almost **1,200%** increase 2014–2024; present in **97.2%** of English local authorities by 2024; **twice** as many per 10,000 in the North as the South (0.6 vs 0.3); **more than three times** as prevalent in the most deprived areas. LDC: **3,573** specialist UK vape shops in 2024 (CSJ citing LDC), above official tobacco-shop counts. ASH: **48%** of current 11–17 vapers bought from shops (GB 2024). Filling a unit is not a health-neutral outcome.
   - **Barbers (GB):** LDC FY2023 — **fastest-growing category**, net **+665** units; density in England & Wales more than doubled over a decade to **3.1 per 10,000**. Labour-intensive, hard to substitute online, typical small-unit occupiers. NHBF: cost and recruitment pressure. CSJ polling: **58%** of Britons say there are “too many” barbers — a **perception** finding, not a productivity finding.
   - **Charity shops (UK):** Charity Retail Association — **9,900+** shops (**~3.3%** of retail units), **24,900+** FTE staff, **223,500+** volunteers, **£300m+** profit to parent charities in 2024/25. Scotland: **900+** shops (CRA). Northern Ireland: about **300** (NICVA/CRA). They occupy voids, sell affordable goods and recycle. They also benefit from **rates relief**, which can crowd other independents and reduce the local tax take even when the unit looks “occupied”.

8. **Implication for Rory:** treat high-street “health” as a **bundle** — vacancy *and* mix *and* catchment income *and* essential services *and* enforcement — not a single vacancy rate. Vape clustering in deprived northern and coastal towns is a **public-health geography** problem. Barber and charity growth is closer to a **resilience** story, with caveats. Councils should use a **repeatable local diagnostic** (section 6) rather than a national average.

## 2. National evidence base

### 2.1 What official statistics can and cannot say

ONS maps **high streets as geographies** (addresses and employment), not shop vacancy. Address class does not equal occupancy. The March 2026 article *High streets and retail areas in Great Britain* is the right official source for **jobs by setting**. Vacancy belongs to **LDC/BRC, Green Street, council Goad/health checks, LPS (NI)**. Footfall belongs to **BRC–Sensormatic, ONS BT (in development), BIDs**.

### 2.2 Vacancy and location type

| Indicator | Figure | Geography / caveat |
|---|---|---|
| High-street vacancy 2023 | 14.0% (from 13.8%) | LDC top 650 GB town centres |
| Overall GB vacancy 2023 | ~14.0–15.3% depending on series | LDC/Green Street |
| Shopping-centre vacancy | Improved in 2023; still ~17–18% in some monitors | Weaker than parks |
| Retail parks | ~3.2% long-term vacancy (LDC 2023) / ~6.8% in some 2024 Knight Frank figures | Different definitions |
| Persistent high-street vacancy | 5.3% of high-street units (2023) | Empty for an extended period |
| Store closures 2024 | 13,479 | CRR, UK-wide |

### 2.3 Employment and mix (ONS, GB, 2015–2024)

Retail employment fell most in **central** settings. Food and accommodation rose on central high streets. That supports a national argument: recovery, where it exists, is **experience- and service-led**, not a return of comparison chains.

### 2.4 Amenity change (England, Health Equity North, 2014–2024)

Using Ordnance Survey Points of Interest, Ghost Towns reports loss of **banks, pharmacies, public toilets, department stores and (in many areas) supermarkets**, and growth of **takeaways and vape shops**, concentrated in **deprived areas and the North**. That is **England**, not UK, and it is **amenity counts**, not vacancy.

### 2.5 Public opinion and “unwanted” uses (CSJ)

CSJ analysis of official business counts: specialist tobacco/vape shops up to almost **2,200** registered, versus LDC **3,573** specialists. Barbers, nails and beauty **+22% since 2016**. Ipsos for CSJ: **68%** say too many vape shops; **58%** too many barbers; **50%** too many nail bars. Useful for **politics of the high street**; not a welfare ranking of those uses.

## 4. The role and limits of vape shops, barbers and charity shops

### 4.1 Argument: they contribute activity, but they are not interchangeable

| | **Vape shops** | **Barbers** | **Charity shops** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What they occupy** | Small Class E / specialist units; also convenience channels | Small service units | Comparison and secondary frontage |
| **Jobs** | Typically few per unit; convenience vape sales sit inside grocery | Labour-intensive; apprenticeships possible; NHBF cost squeeze | 24,900+ FTE UK plus 223,500+ volunteers |
| **Footfall type** | Repeat adult (and, illegally, youth) purchases; short dwell | Appointment / walk-in; social “third place” | Browse, donate, Gift Aid; value shoppers |
| **Resilience** | Vulnerable to licensing, display rules, disposable ban (Jun 2025), illicit trade | Resilient to e-commerce; exposed to wages/NICs/rent | Rates relief + donations; NIC/cost pressure; rag market |
| **Health / social** | Adult cessation tool (ASH) **and** youth/illicit harm; North/deprivation gradient | Community contact; CSJ “too many” perception | Affordable goods, reuse, charity surplus (~£300m 2024/25) |
| **Planning** | England: government signalling planning permission / licensing for new vape shops | Usually Class E | Class E; cannot easily “cap” numbers |

**Do not claim** that more barbers or charity shops “cause” high-street recovery. They are **associated** with occupied frontage and, for charity retail, measured social surplus. **Do not claim** that vape shops “destroy” high streets. They **correlate** with vacancy-filling in weak markets and with deprivation. Causal claims need local before/after studies that public data do not yet provide.

### 4.2 Vape shops — contribution and constraint

**Contribution:** they pay rent on units that comparison chains have left; they may support adult switching from smoking if they operate as specialist, age-restricted retailers (ASH’s caveat against treating all vape shops as “rogue”). Scotland’s register shows nicotine retail is much larger than specialist-shop counts: **5,573 to 7,069** registered outlets 2020–2024, concentrated in deprived places.

**Constraint:** England amenity research shows **northern and deprived** over-provision. Trading Standards in the North East and West Midlands document underage sales and illicit product. CSJ and HEN frame clustering with takeaways and betting as an **unhealthy high street**. Occupancy gained this way can **worsen** the health mission of town-centre policy.

### 4.3 Barbers — contribution and constraint

**Contribution:** GB’s fastest-growing retail category in LDC 2023; density doubling is a **structural** services shift, visible from Elgin to inner Birmingham. They generate frequent local trips and micro-employment. Mansfield’s monitoring shows change-of-use to barbers as a real local mechanism.

**Constraint:** CSJ groups barbers with nails as “unwanted” in public opinion. Saturation on secondary streets (Morriston: seven barbers on one strip) does not recreate a comparison offer. NHBF surveys show weak hiring intentions. Some enforcement cases link a minority of premises to illicit tobacco — **do not smear the whole trade**.

### 4.4 Charity shops — contribution and constraint

**Contribution:** clearest **UK-wide quantified** package: shops, jobs, volunteers, surplus to parent charities, waste reduction polling. In weaker Welsh valleys, Scottish towns and NI high streets they are often the remaining national “brand” on the street. CRA argues they reduce vacancy (~3.3% of units).

**Constraint:** HEN records **falling** charity-shop provision per 10,000 in England 2014–2024 even as the national stock remains large — consolidation, not endless expansion. Rates relief is politically contested. Civil Society surveys show **profit compression**. A street of charity shops can mean **affordable access** or **failed commercial demand**. Local audits should count them as a **use class of interest**, not automatically as success or failure.

## 5. Region-by-region notes (with towns and cities)

### 5.1 North East England

**Headline:** GB’s highest tracked vacancy and some of England’s sharpest amenity loss, with extreme town-level outliers.

County Durham 2025: **19.1%** town-centre vacancy (target 13.9%); **Peterlee 40.8%**, **Bishop Auckland 35.4%**. PwC 2024: **502 closures vs 359 openings** (net −143), better than 2023 but still negative. HEN: NE takeaways **+35%**, banks **−30%**, pharmacies **−16%**, toilets **−32%**, vapes from near-zero to **~0.5 per 10,000**. CSJ ranks the NE poorly on combined “social breakdown” scores; Hartlepool cited around **18%** retail vacancy in that work.

**Places:** Newcastle (stronger core; east high streets investment plan); Gateshead (~29% retail vacancy in Task Force-type reviews; leakage to Newcastle); Sunderland (city-centre vacancy reported above NE and UK averages; High Street West chain loss); Middlesbrough (explicit oversupply / consolidation strategy).

**Sectors:** TSNE seized **1.4 tonnes** of illegal disposables (2022 ops) and found **44%** of 32 test-purchased retailers sold to an underage volunteer. Barbers: local qualitative anchors, no NE census. Charity: UK CRA figures only.

### 5.2 North West England

**Headline:** Polarised — Manchester and parts of Liverpool vs Lancashire, coastal and district centres.

LDC **15.6%** end-2023 (largest regional improvement in that commentary, partly Manchester/Trafford). Persistent vacancy **6.9%**. PwC 2024: **956 openings / 1,311 closures**. HEN: takeaways **+33%**; northern vape gradient. Blackburn, Preston, Blackpool, Bolton appear in national vape-density lists (press on HEN).

**Places:** Manchester (events/footfall; district-centre programmes); Liverpool (regeneration narrative; third-party vacancy estimates weaker than Manchester); Preston 35 plan; Blackpool Growth and Prosperity / BID; Wigan **32.3%** units during Galleries works; Burnley (Long-Term Plan for Towns).

### 5.3 Yorkshire and the Humber

**Headline:** Leeds and York hold up; Bradford, Sheffield and Hull carry leakage and vacancy.

PwC 2024 net **−325** chain units. Centre for Cities: Bradford **~18%** (second after Newport in that set); York **~9.2%**; Sheffield **~12.9%** among large centres. Leeds 12.8m Briggate visits 2023; 2024 cameras **+1.3%**. York BID July 2025 footfall **−13.7%** YoY. Hull: 34+ visible empties on core streets (2024 press count). HEN: amenities **−29%**; vapes **0.5/10k**.

**Places:** Leeds (regional consumption hub); Bradford (City Village / culture-led shrink of retail); Sheffield vs Meadowhall; York (visitor ~40% of centre spend); Hull Community Highstreets Programme; Wakefield/Huddersfield leakage to Leeds.

### 5.4 East Midlands

**Headline:** Large city cores above UK vacancy benchmarks; some district centres healthier.

Nottingham **21.1%** units (16.3% ex-Broadmarsh); Leicester **22.1%** / **23.1%** comparison floorspace vs UK **13.9%**; Derby **18.2%**, footfall **~60m (2024)** vs **65.5m (2019)**; Mansfield **17.4%** (2024, up from 13.9%); Newark **12.63%**; Northampton primary area **~15%**. Out-of-centre: Fosse Park, Rushden Lakes, Milton Keynes.

**Sectors:** Charity UK-wide. Barbers documented in Mansfield change-of-use. Vape: ASH-CRUK — **8/8** East Midlands councils in a regional tobacco-control programme; **83%** of English LAs did underage test purchase (England, not EM-only).

### 5.5 West Midlands

**Headline:** Patchwork — distressed industrial cores versus tighter county towns and some Birmingham districts.

Hanley **30.4%** units (July 2025); Wolverhampton **25.4%**; Walsall PSA **25.3%**; Coventry prime **16.0%**, City Centre South **46.6%** (regeneration distortion); Worcester **~10%**; Birmingham districts such as Acocks Green **7.69%** (2025). CRR UK closures apply as context, not a WM total.

**Sectors:** Operation-scale vape seizures (Birmingham, Sandwell, Walsall). Barbers: GB LDC, inferred locally. Charity: UK CRA; one Telford raid touching a charity shop shows enforcement is not format-blind.

### 5.6 East of England

**Headline:** Historically low-vacancy region with recently weak footfall and stressed Suffolk/Essex cores.

Norwich retail unit vacancy **15.2%** (Sep 2025 monitor). Cambridge **13.8%** city-wide vacant units but Main Centre **7.7%**; Grafton area **40.7%** (redevelopment). Peterborough **11–12%** (council). Ipswich central **19.0%** vacant units. Colchester: persistent voids / oversupply narrative. Luton CoStar **~2.89%** is a **database outlier**, not a Goad equivalent.

**Sectors:** Cambridge charity shops **6.1%** of comparison retailers vs **9.7%** nationwide. Barbers named in Colchester evidence as non-online services.

### 5.7 London

**Headline:** Lowest regional vacancy; two-speed activity (suburban local vs central destination).

LDC Greater London **10.6%** (Q4 2023). Centre for Cities: London city centre **~7%**; **78%** of suburban high streets beat UK city-centre vacancy average. Consumption area A (Uxbridge, Twickenham) **6–8%**; area B (Stamford Hill, West Hackney, Finsbury Park) **10–12%**. TfL central pedestrian **~90% of 2019** (Jul–Sep 2024). Some central BIDs report much larger gaps (Heart of London **39%** below pre-pandemic — BID geography, not all London).

**Sectors:** City of London **4.6** vape shops per 10,000 (2024) — inflated by tiny resident population. Dense barber market. Charity chains on high-value streets under UK cost pressure (Civil Society: profits down **16%** among comparable 2024 survey respondents — UK, not London-only).

### 5.8 South East England

**Headline:** Affluent primes look tight; Reading and some coastal/restructuring towns do not.

Flude 2025 agent vacancy: Worthing **1.4%**, Winchester **2.0%**, Southampton **2.1%**, Brighton & Hove **2.2%**, Chichester **2.6%**, Guildford **4.4%**, Portsmouth **4.6%** — **not** ONS. Reading Goad May 2024: **18.6%** units, **27.1%** floorspace. Brighton BID: **9.5%** ground-floor city-wide / **7.5%** BID (different metric). Hastings LDC commercial vacancy **11.7%** vs England **10.8%** (Nov 2023, withdrawn MHCLG profile — dated).

**Sectors:** Brighton closure order on illegal vapes/tobacco (Lewes Road, 2024). ADPH South East position on regulated vaping vs illicit trade. Buckinghamshire “health on the high street” (Aylesbury Unit 33) as a **purposeful** alternative use.

### 5.9 South West England

**Headline:** Mid-ranked large centres; leakage and weekday geography matter as much as vacancy.

Centre for Cities 2025 estimates: Exeter **~10.5%**, Bournemouth **11.2%**, Plymouth **11.4%**, Bristol **11.6%**, Swindon **12.5%**, Gloucester **13.9%**. Bristol spend leakage to Cribbs Causeway; Broadmead/Galleries historically far worse (21.7% units in Oct 2021 survey). Plymouth attributes improvement to F&B/leisure and public realm. Swindon: suburban employment parks weaken weekday core. Bath: Vacant Units Action Project. Gloucester: High Street Rental Auction powers.

### 5.10 Wales

**Headline:** Structurally high vacancy and falling footfall; Cardiff as the consumption hub.

WRC/Welsh Government: **one in six** empty; persistent **7.7%** (2023); Q3 2023 all-location **16.6%**; footfall still **~12%** below pre-pandemic in that Senedd evidence. 2024 footfall **−2.9%**. Retail jobs **118,000** (2023 WG). Centre for Cities: Newport **19%** (highest of 62); Cardiff **12.4%**; Swansea **15.4%**. Wrexham year-end **15.22%**. Carmarthen **18–19%** (Mar 2024 LDO). Denbighshire footfall **−2.0%** 2025 vs 2024.

**Sectors:** Morriston (Swansea) — five vape shops, seven barbers, nine takeaways on the main street (councillor evidence, 2025). ASH Wales: licensing and density near schools. Charity: dense hospice/CRW networks in valleys towns. Transforming Towns / Town Centre First / 2026 taskforce (Bangor, Bridgend pilots).

### 5.11 Scotland

**Headline:** Average conceals Aberdeen/Ayrshire stress and island/small-town tightness.

Council-reported town-centre vacancy **12.3%** (2023–24), up from 11.4% two years earlier. Extremes: South Ayrshire **35.3%**, Aberdeen City **20.7%**, Fife **18.8%**, Western Isles **3.3%**. SRC/LDC Q2 2023 **15.9%**. Footfall **−2.0%** in 2024. Glasgow council **12.1%** but 2024 visitor/sales wobble. Dundee **17.7%**. Inverness tracker **~8.1%** vs Highland 2022 health check **12.6%** (definition clash). Borders summer 2025 **12%**.

**Sectors:** **900+** charity shops. NVP **register** (not shop count) **7,069** (2024). Elgin: 20 hairdresser/barber units, about half of retail-service outlets (2025). ASH Scotland: conditional registration and TS resource.

### 5.12 Northern Ireland

**Headline:** Different statistics; high all-property vacancy; sharp core–fringe splits; rates politics.

LPS covers **all non-domestic**, not shops. Newtownards study: **22.8%** average across 41 towns (Oct 2024), **34.3%** Belfast, **18.8%** excluding Belfast. BBC/LPS: **1,914 / 5,576** vacant in Belfast city boundary (31 Oct 2024). NIRC footfall **−2.2%** in 2024; Belfast December **−7.2%** after a strong event-led summer. Derry 2021: **13.8%** of 450 units. Newry 2022: **21.5%**. Newtownards 2025: town **21.3%** but High Street core **5.2%**.

**Sectors:** **~300** charity shops; rates exemption debate. Barbers in Vacant to Vibrant backfill. Disposable vape ban from **1 June 2025**; Tobacco and Vapes Act licensing to follow. GB CRR closure totals **must not** be read as NI.

## 6. A practical diagnostic for every town or city

Apply the same questions locally. If a data item is missing, record **“unknown”** — do not impute the regional average.

1. **Vacancy, three ways:** unit %; floorspace %; persistent (e.g. 2–3 years). Separate **regeneration voids** (Coventry City Centre South, Wigan Galleries, Cambridge Grafton, Nottingham Broadmarsh).
2. **Catchment economics:** resident incomes; **spend leakage** to a larger city or mall (Bradford→Leeds, Sheffield→Meadowhall, Bristol→Cribbs, Newport→Cardiff, Northampton→MK/Rushden Lakes).
3. **Essential services:** banks, pharmacies, GPs, toilets, supermarket access (HEN checklist) — not just “shops open”.
4. **Mix quality:** share of comparison / convenience / food / personal services / **vape** / **charity** / betting / vacant. Flag **monoculture** (Morriston-type strips).
5. **Footfall by daypart:** weekday vs Saturday vs evening (hybrid-work cities vs tourist vs commuter suburbs).
6. **Enforcement load:** underage vape test-purchase fail rate; illicit seizures; shoplifting/ASB (CSJ/NIRC themes).
7. **Employment:** high-street jobs by industry if BRES/ONS local extracts exist; otherwise BID/chamber surveys.
8. **Policy tools in use:** High Street Rental Auctions, BID, Town Deal / Transforming Towns / Vacant to Vibrant, meanwhile uses, residential conversion, health hubs.
9. **Sector-specific tests:**
   - Vape: specialist vs convenience; proximity to schools; licensing readiness.
   - Barber: unit count vs population; apprenticeship take-up; not “too many” as the only KPI.
   - Charity: count, volunteer hours, whether rates relief is crowding independents **in that pitch**.
10. **Decision rule:** if vacancy is falling **only** because of vape/takeaway clustering while pharmacies/banks fall, score the centre as **occupancy up, health down**.

## 7. Implications

### Councils and combined authorities

- Stop using a single vacancy rate as the success metric.
- Pair **Town Centre First / mixed-use shrink** (Wales, Scotland, NE CA, Bradford City Village) with **licensing and planning** on nicotine retail where statute allows.
- Fund **Trading Standards** if vape policy tightens — otherwise illicit trade fills the same units.
- Publish **comparable annual health checks** (unit, floorspace, persistent, mix) so towns can be compared inside the authority, as Newark, Mansfield, Norwich and Moray already do.

### Landlords and investors

- Prime London, South East historic cores, Cambridge, York, parts of Edinburgh/Glasgow and Cardiff still clear on occupancy; **secondary and large-format** stock is the UK problem (Reading Oracle, Hanley, Union Street, Broadmarsh).
- Service and F&B tenants can re-base income; they do not automatically restore rateable-value growth.
- Charity and vape covenants have **reputational and regulatory** tails.

### Businesses and BIDs

- Barbers and charity retail are **allies for daytime animation**, not a full strategy.
- Events and evening economy explain Belfast summer / Manchester / Leeds outliers — they are **not** transferable to every market town without catchment spend.

## 8. Data cadence and the quarterly tracker

### 8.1 The cadence problem

The catchment-spend evidence in this briefing is **not on a recurring cycle**, and should not be treated as a live indicator.

- **Centre for Cities, *Checking out*** — published **10 July 2025** as a one-off report. Companion catchment data tool **last updated 17 September 2025**. No stated refresh cycle.
- **Second caveat:** the vacancy rates in that report are **modelled, not counted**. Only **37 of the 63** cities supplied empty-property data; the remainder were estimated by regression using rateable values, change in high-street units since 2010, and earlier Local Data Company data. Acceptable for a one-off narrative; **not** suitable as a quarterly metric.
- **ONS × Visa card spending flow** (cardholder-to-merchant flows by postal district — the closest free proxy for leakage) — latest release **25 March 2024**, covering only to **Q3 2023**; next release **"to be announced"**. Indexed to Q1 2019 = 100, not absolute pounds. The Visa feed *is* refreshed quarterly to ONS and the methodology note (revised 8 January 2026) covers 2019–2025, but publication frequency is listed as *"recurring, to be confirmed"*.

**Decision:** this tracker uses **free sources only** — ONS, councils, LPS, GOV.UK, and property-house research notes that are published without a paywall. **Catchment spend is not a live KPI** on that constraint. Keep Centre for Cities as a one-off argument, not a quarterly series. Paid feeds (Beauclair, CACI, BRC member monitors, CRA QMA full reports) are out of scope.

### 8.2 Free stack (what we will actually refresh)

| Source | Cadence | What it gives | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ONS UK retail footfall** (BT Active Intelligence) | Weekly / monthly XLSX; latest 27 Aug 2026 | GB region + site type (town/city, district, retail park) | Official statistics in development; not seasonally adjusted; **NI suppressed** |
| **ONS real-time indicators bulletin** | Weekly | Narrative regional footfall | Same caveats |
| **ONS high streets and retail areas** | Periodic (latest Mar 2026) | GB/regional **employment** on high streets 2015–2024 | Not vacancy; not quarterly |

| **PwC store openings and closures** | Annual press release | Regional net chain change | Multiples only; not independents |

### 8.4 What a free “quarterly” pack actually contains

Each calendar quarter, download and file:

1. **ONS footfall XLSX** — last three months vs same quarter previous year, by GB region and by town/city vs retail park. Do not rank regions on one wet month.
2. **LPS NI CSV** — non-domestic vacancy by district; footnote that it is not shop-only.
3. **Any new Savills / Knight Frank / Green Street public note** — GB headline vacancy only.
4. **Any new council health check** in the contrast set (search `[town] town centre health check vacancy PDF`).
5. **CRA public stats page** — charity shop UK totals if updated.
6. **One FOI or licensing snapshot** only if a council has published new vape/enforcement numbers.

That is a **quarterly national pulse plus an irregular town ledger**. It is not a UK-wide quarterly vacancy series, and it is not catchment spend.

## 9. Limitations

- **No UK-wide official vacancy series** covering every town; LDC is top 650 GB centres; NI uses LPS.
- **Methods clash:** Goad, council walking surveys, Centre for Cities modelled rates, agent opinions, LPS ratings — never add them into one index without a footnote.
- **Sector counts:** vape specialists (LDC) ≠ tobacco SIC counts ≠ Scotland NVP register. Charity CRA “find a shop” ≠ HEN POI time series.
- **2024–26 footfall** is noisy (weather, ONS short series).
- **Cadence:** free sources give monthly GB footfall and quarterly NI vacancy. Catchment spend and a UK-wide regional shop-vacancy table are not available without paying. Centre for Cities is one-off and partly modelled (section 8).
- **No causal model** in this briefing linking vape/barber/charity density to vacancy change.
- Towns named are **illustrative**, not a ranking of every place in the region.
